How we built Dream, and why.

Dream tests one idea: what makes Indian roads hard for driving software can be measured from dashcam footage, and once it is measured, software can practise against it before it meets the real road. This page sets out the data, the method and the results, including what did not work and what we have not shown yet.

The thesis

The hard part is how often things happen.

Most driving software learns and is tested on orderly roads. Indian roads are not orderly, but they are not random either. Two-wheelers squeeze past every minute or so, people cross wherever they need to, autos cut in, and now and then a vehicle comes the wrong way. Each of these happens at some rate, and a rate is something you can count.

We collect dashcam footage on Indian roads for a living. So we asked four questions. Can our footage alone tell us how often each of these things happens? Can a simulator reproduce those rates? Does it change how driving software does? And can other teams use the result as it is? The sections below take them in turn.

1 · The footage and the labels

100 random clips, one main event each.

We drew 100 random windows from two of our drives through Gurgaon, one by day and one at night, all from the front camera. Each clip is 6 seconds: the labeller names the main event that starts in the middle 4 seconds, and the second either side is context. One person on our team labelled all 100 with a fixed list of events (taxonomy v0.4) and this rule, word for word:

Label only what starts in the highlighted middle 4 s; the first and last second are context. If several things start there, pick the highest one in this list (others before ego, closer calls first). Pick 'nothing' when nothing notable starts.

Six of the 100 clips, chosen to show different events. Faces, number plates and painted numbers are blurred, and the dashcam's own overlay is cropped off. Under each: our label, and what the best AI model said about the same clip.

2 · Could AI do the labelling?

Not yet. The best model barely beats answering "nothing".

Labelling by hand does not scale, so we gave five off-the-shelf AI video models the same 100 clips, the same list and the same rule. The models miss routine events most: overtakes, and two-wheelers squeezing past. So every rate in Dream comes from the human labels, and AI labelling we can trust is part of our next work.

3 · From labels to rates

Counts, rates and how sure we are.

100 clips of 4 seconds are 400 seconds of driving, about 0.11 hours. A rate is the number of clips whose main event is X, divided by that time. With counts this small the uncertainty is large, so each rate comes with an exact 95% interval for a count of rare events (Poisson). A clip with two events counts only its main one, so these rates are lower bounds.

4 · The simulator

A city that keeps those rates.

  1. Map. Every road, junction and signal in a stretch of Gurgaon about 5 km by 2 km, from OpenStreetMap, cut at the box we downloaded, with a wall at its edge.
  2. Traffic. Each vehicle keeps its gap with the Intelligent Driver Model and changes lanes with MOBIL, the standard models in traffic research. On top of them, Indian behaviour: two-wheelers filter through gaps and ride lane lines, vehicles take turns at junctions without signals, autos stop for passengers and buses stop at bus stops.
  3. Road surface. Speed breakers and bus stops from OpenStreetMap where it maps them, the rest assumed; potholes assumed.
  4. Events. A generator adds what the traffic does not produce on its own. It schedules each event at the rate still missing, divided by the share of its attempts that play out (some find no chance, or are called off), so the total lands on the measured rate.
  5. Records. Every event says whether the traffic produced it or the generator added it, so anyone can separate the two.

The same kind of event on the road and in Dream. Dream is drawn from above, while our clips come from the dashcam: giving driving software the camera's view of Dream is the next step.

Limits

What Dream does not show yet.

  • Driving software here sees positions, not pictures. It gets an exact list of the road users around it. A real car sees camera images. Dream tests the traffic, not the eyes.
  • The sample is small and has one labeller. 100 clips give wide ranges, and with one labeller we cannot yet say how much two people would agree.
  • Two drives, one stretch of one city. Both drives are in Gurgaon, and the map is part of Gurgaon.
  • Simulated cars stand still too often, partly because of the cautious planner we tested with and partly because of how junctions without signals clear.
  • Some numbers are assumed, as listed above, and the code marks each one.

What comes next

Driving software that sees what a car sees.

The next step closes the biggest gap above: an environment where the software receives camera images from our footage, as it would on the road. First, it watches real frames from our four-camera rig, plans the next few seconds, and we score that plan against what actually happened. Then a world model paints the camera view of Dream's scenes, so the software drives in closed loop and the traffic reacts to it. Alongside: more labelled footage, a second labeller, and AI labelling we can trust.

Dream is where this work will live as it lands: training environments, world models and the benchmarks behind them. If you would like to help label footage, test your own planner, or need data from the roads you ship into, we would like to hear from you.

Reproduce it

Every number on this page comes from code in the repository.

pip install "thirdeyelabs[sim]"

# the realism check: Dream counted the way the clips were labelled, against our footage
python -m thirdeye_sim.realism --drives 48

# the planner benchmark: 100 seeded routes in orderly and in Gurgaon traffic
python -m thirdeye_sim.evaluate --episodes 100

Code Apache-2.0 at github.com/thirdeyelabsai/thirdeye-sim. The footage summaries the checks compare against ship with the package (CC BY 4.0); the clips themselves stay with us.
